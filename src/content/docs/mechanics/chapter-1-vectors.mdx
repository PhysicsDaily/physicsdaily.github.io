## Concept & Definition

Tell someone a car is moving at $60\ \text{km/h}$ and you have only told them *how fast*. Tell them it is moving at $60\ \text{km/h}$ **due north** and you have told them what actually matters. Many physical quantities — displacement, velocity, acceleration, force — need this second piece of information, and quantities that carry it are called **vectors**.

## Scalars and Vectors

Physical quantities fall into two families, depending on what it takes to describe them completely:

- A **scalar** has magnitude only. Mass, time, temperature, distance, and energy are scalars.
- A **vector** has both magnitude and direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, and force are vectors.

We write a vector with an arrow, such as $\vec{v}$, and draw it as an arrow whose **length** represents the magnitude and whose **arrowhead** points in the direction. The magnitude alone is written $|\vec{v}|$ and is always a non-negative scalar.

<Callout type="definition" title="Scalar vs. Vector">
A scalar is fully described by a number and a unit. A vector also needs a direction.
</Callout>

## Components

A vector drawn on a plane can be split into two perpendicular parts: one along the $x$-axis and one along the $y$-axis. These are its **components**, $A_x$ and $A_y$. Each component is a signed number — positive when it points along the positive axis, negative when it points against it.

To keep track of direction, we attach a **unit vector** to each axis: $\hat{i}$ points along $+x$ and $\hat{j}$ along $+y$, each with magnitude one. Multiplying a component by its unit vector turns a plain number back into a vector along that axis.

<FormulaCard title="Vector Components in 2D" label="Key Formula">

$$
\vec{A} = A_x\,\hat{i} + A_y\,\hat{j}
$$

where $A_x$ and $A_y$ are the components of $\vec{A}$ and $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ are unit vectors along the $x$- and $y$-axes.

</FormulaCard>

The two components and the vector itself form a right triangle, so the magnitude follows directly from the Pythagorean theorem:

<FormulaCard title="Magnitude of a Vector" label="Key Formula">

$$
|\vec{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}
$$

where $A_x$ and $A_y$ are the components of $\vec{A}$ along the $x$- and $y$-axes.

</FormulaCard>

## Direction and Resolution

The same right triangle gives the direction. If $\theta$ is the angle the vector makes with the positive $x$-axis, the components can be recovered from the magnitude and the angle:

<FormulaCard title="Resolving a Vector" label="Key Formula">

$$
A_x = |\vec{A}|\cos\theta, \qquad A_y = |\vec{A}|\sin\theta
$$

where $|\vec{A}|$ is the magnitude of $\vec{A}$ and $\theta$ is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.

</FormulaCard>

Going the other way, the angle is found from the ratio of the components:

<FormulaCard title="Direction of a Vector" label="Key Formula">

$$
\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)
$$

where $A_x$ and $A_y$ are the components of $\vec{A}$ and $\theta$ is its angle from the positive $x$-axis.

</FormulaCard>

<Callout type="warning">
A calculator returns $\tan^{-1}$ only between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, so it cannot tell $(3, 4)$ from $(-3, -4)$. Always check the signs of $A_x$ and $A_y$ to place the vector in the correct quadrant before trusting the angle.
</Callout>

Components let us analyze each direction independently — the idea that makes kinematics and Newton's laws tractable. A problem in a plane becomes two simpler problems along straight lines.

<QuickCheck question="A vector has components A_x = 3 and A_y = 4. What is its magnitude?">

Apply the magnitude formula: $|\vec{A}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

</QuickCheck>
